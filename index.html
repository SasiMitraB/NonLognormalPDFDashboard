Non-lognormal density probability distribution and turbulent Mach numbers in the local Interstellar Medium from three-dimensional data

The probability distribution function (PDF) of gas density is a key tool used to study turbulence and star formation in the interstellar medium (ISM). It is usually modelled as a combination of a turbulent part, modelled as a lognormal or non-lognormal distribution, and a gravitational part as a power law. Related observational studies have been limited to 2D column-density maps, which are affected by projection, and 3D simulations usually involve approximations. Here, we use the parsec-scale three-dimensional dust map of the local ISM ($\le 1250\,\mathrm{pc}$) to measure, for the first time, the volumetric density PDF of individual dense clouds directly in 3D.

We identify $766$ clouds using the dendrogram algorithm, and study their turbulent envelopes and fit each cloud's density PDF with turbulence and gravitational parts. Of the $475$ clouds successfully fit ($62\%$), an overwhelming number of clouds show preference for the non-lognormal Castaing-Hopkins models ($76\%$ of fitted clouds, compared to $24\%$ for lognormal models), with a substantial fraction requiring non-zero intermittency. We report this as the first observational evidence for non-lognormal density PDFs in the ISM. We derive the turbulent Mach number, $\mathcal{M}$, for all model classes, and find the local ISM is dominated by transonic to mildly supersonic turbulence ($\mathcal{M} = 1.1$–$3.6$). Moreover, we demonstrate that 2D column-density PDFs fail to capture the 3D information and thus care must be taken to derive 3D physical quantities of interest from 2D column-density maps.

1Theoretical framework and interactive PDF simulator

We model the volume-weighted PDF of the logarithm of the normalised density, $$s = \ln(\rho / \rho_0)$$ where $\rho$ is the local density and $\rho_0$ is the mean density of the cloud. The models are either turbulent (lognormal, $\mathcal{LN}$, or non-lognormal Castaing-Hopkins, $\mathcal{CH}$) or combine a turbulent part with a gravitational power-law tail ($\mathcal{PL}$).
